# TIFA 0.4.7

Released 2026-11-05 (tag `v0.4.7`). [All releases](../news.md).

Version 0.4.7 gathers the work below since 0.4.6. SIQS is 1.1 to 4.1 times
as fast from 232 to 280 bits on an Apple M5 Pro, the same 2 balanced
semiprimes per size timed with both versions:

| `siqs()`, bits | 232 | 240 | 248 | 256 | 264 | 272 | 280 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.4.6 | 5.77 s | 9.38 s | 18.98 s | 41.36 s | 67.26 s | 198.5 s | 406.6 s |
| 0.4.7 | 5.27 s | 8.93 s | 13.85 s | 22.75 s | 31.44 s | 61.0 s | 98.0 s |
| ratio | 0.91 | 0.95 | 0.73 | 0.55 | 0.47 | 0.31 | 0.24 |

Below 232 bits nothing changes but the layout of the code: SIQS takes 0.98
to 1.01 of the time of 0.4.6 from 70 to 220 bits (`bench/ab.sh`), and
`tifa_factor()` 1.005 to 1.02 from 80 to 160 bits; builds in between, with
no change to that code, varied from 1.00 to 1.03. Also new: primality
certificates, time limits, `tifa-tune` for large sizes, the Python
package's proofs, time limits and tuning files, and the fixes of a review
for untrusted input. The ABI only grows: the shared library is still
`libtifa.so.0.4`.

## 5 November 2026 &mdash; Python: time limits and tuning files

- `factor(n, timeout=60)` raises `tifa.FactorizationTimeout` (a
  `TimeoutError`, and a `FactorizationError` holding the factors found) if
  `n` is not factored within about a minute; `prime_certificate(p,
  timeout=60)` raises `TimeoutError`. See the time limits below.
- `set_tuning_dir(path)` takes the parameters of the algorithms from the
  tuning files of `path`, for the whole process (`None` goes back to the
  `TIFA_TUNING_DIR` environment variable, `""` to the built-in parameters
  alone); the wheels install `tifa-tune`, which writes such files.

## 5 November 2026 &mdash; Time limits

`tifa_set_time_limit(seconds)` (`time_limit.h`, which `tifa.h` includes)
limits the time of the calling thread's factorizations: `tifa_factor()`,
`tifa_prove_prime()` and the algorithms that take long give up when they
next check the time, and return what they found. SIQS checks it every 16
chunks of its sieve, its elimination every 256 columns, ECM after each
curve, and the factoring modes other than `SINGLE_RUN` before each new run.
`tifa_time_limit_reached()` tells whether it has passed. Each thread has
its own limit.

On an Apple M5 Pro, factorizations of 240 to 280 bits stopped within a
millisecond of their limits (from 0.5 s to 60 s), in SIQS's collection of
relations as in ECM's search for a factor of 80 or 90 bits (with the hint
`unbalanced`); in the elimination, which checks every 256 columns, it can be
a few tens of milliseconds at 280 bits. Time of the new version over that of
the previous one, without a limit:

| `siqs()`, bits | 70 | 100 | 140 | 180 | 220 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apple M5 Pro | 0.990 | 0.997 | 1.004 | 0.997 | 0.999 |

and 0.994 to 1.003 for `tifa_factor()` (100 and 140 bits, and 160 bits with
a factor of 40 bits) and ECM (100 bits); with a limit that does not expire,
`tifa.factor()` takes as long at 180 bits. The ABI only grows: the shared
library is still `libtifa.so.0.4`.

## 5 November 2026 &mdash; tifa-tune: large sizes

`tifa-tune` can tune SIQS up to 320 bits (200 before), where a run takes
minutes and the best parameters differ between processors (see
[Large SIQS sizes](../README.md#large-siqs-sizes)):

- `--sizes <list>` tunes a list of bit-sizes rather than a range.
- `--search descent` starts from the built-in defaults and tries each value
  of one parameter at a time, the others at their best yet, for at most two
  passes: 14 combinations per pass for SIQS rather than the grid's 100. SIQS
  then also tries its sieve threshold 3 and 6 lower and 3 higher.
- `--fit <bits>` writes a row every `<bits>` bits from the smallest tuned
  size to the largest, each a least-squares quadratic on the 5 nearest tuned
  sizes (on the logarithm of factor bases, bounds and widths), kept within
  their values; the tuned rows are kept as comments.
- By default, the watchdog no longer kills a run after 30 s, which SIQS runs
  take from about 264 bits on an Apple M5 Pro: the defaults' runs have no
  limit, then the others have 10 times the defaults' longest, and at least
  30 s.
- The grid times the built-in defaults first, so that the others are cut
  off against them from the start.
- A grid point of SIQS or CFRAC with a larger factor base than the
  defaults' trial-divided by the defaults' primes only, and factored the rest
  with Bernstein's batch algorithm: slower than the library, which
  trial-divides by the whole base, so `tifa-tune` underrated larger bases.

## 5 November 2026 &mdash; SIQS: narrower sieves from 248 bits

The sieve half widths of the fitted parameters (below) were too wide on
most processors: at 264 bits, the AMD EPYC 9V74 (Zen 4) and 9V45 (Zen 5)
were fastest with a quarter to a third of them, an Intel Xeon Platinum
8573C with half or less. They are now halved from 248 to 280 bits, and 0.7
of the fit's from 288 bits, where half was slower on an Apple M5 Pro. Time
of halved widths over that of the fit's, `siqs-bench` on the same balanced
semiprimes for both (2 per size to 280 bits, then 1; three EPYC 7763
runners):

| `siqs()`, bits | 244 | 248 | 256 | 264 | 272 | 280 | 288 | 296 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apple M5 Pro | 0.94 | 0.91 | 0.89 | 0.93 | 0.92 | 0.96 | 1.01 | 1.03 |
| Intel Xeon Platinum 8573C | 0.91 | 0.81 | 0.74 | 0.76 | 0.75 | 0.76 | 0.77 | 0.75 |
| AMD EPYC 7763 (Zen 3) | 0.97&ndash;0.98 | 0.97&ndash;0.98 | 0.95&ndash;0.98 | 1.02&ndash;1.03 | 1.05 | 1.08&ndash;1.09 | 1.11&ndash;1.12 | 1.13&ndash;1.14 |

With 0.7 of the fit's widths from 288 bits, the M5 takes 0.99 of the fit's
time at 288 and 296 bits. On the EPYC 7763, wider widths remain faster from
264 bits: `tifa-tune` can find them for such a machine (see above). The
cores disagree among themselves, not by architecture, so a second table for
x86-64 would not help. The EPYC 7763 has the smallest L2 cache per core of
them (512 KB): a possible reason, not verified.

## 5 November 2026 &mdash; Tuning tables apply near their rows only

`tifa_tuning_row_for()` and `tifa_tuning_get_row()` gave the first or last
row of a table for any size below or above it: with a SIQS table from
`tifa-tune`'s default range, 65 to 200 bits, a 288-bit number got the
200-bit row, 4600 primes in the base and a sieve half width of 196608,
instead of the 65536 primes and 914000 of the built-in table. A table now
applies from 8 bits below its first row to 8 bits above its last, and the
algorithm's built-in defaults beyond. `tifa_tuning_lookup()` and
`tifa_tuning_interpolate()`, on a table, still clamp.

## 5 November 2026 &mdash; SIQS: parameters from 232 bits

The SIQS parameters above 210 bits were a crude extrapolation, never
measured past the old cap of the factor base (below), and absent above 280
bits. A coordinate descent at 272 and 288 bits on an Apple M5 Pro, on the
size of the base, the sieve half width and the threshold, found the half
widths 3 to 4 times too wide (272 bits: 85.1 s to 62.9 s; 288 bits: 267.8 s
to 186.4 s). From 232 bits, the parameters now come from least-squares
quadratics in the size of n, fitted on the parameters from 140 to 200 bits
and on these two: of the logarithm of the factor base, of the logarithm of
the half width (kept at its peak, at 268 bits, above), and of the threshold.
One row every 8 bits up to 320, the base stopping at 65536 primes. Time
over that of the two tuned rows alone, `siqs-bench` on balanced semiprimes
(on the M5, A/B over four rounds up to 236 bits, 2 numbers each above):

| `siqs()`, bits | 232 | 240 | 248 | 256 | 264 | 272 | 288 | 296 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apple M5 Pro | 0.93 | 0.98 | 0.96 | 0.90 | 0.86 | 1.00 | 0.99 | 0.95 |
| Intel Xeon Platinum 8573C | | | 0.91 | 0.80 | 0.61 | | | |
| AMD EPYC 9V74 (Zen 4) | | | | 0.86 | 0.61 | | | |
| AMD EPYC 7763 (Zen 3) | | | 1.00 | 0.97 | 0.90 | | | |

Up to 225 bits the parameters stay: there, the fit's wider half widths took
1.06 to 1.18 of their time (212 to 222 bits).

## 4 November 2026 &mdash; SIQS: factor bases of up to 65536 primes

The factor base of SIQS was capped at a quarter of the 65536 tabulated
primes, 16384, while its parameters asked for 22520 primes at 248 bits,
38280 at 264 and 65250 from 280: above about 240 bits, SIQS sieved with too
few primes and found its relations slowly. Past the table, the primes now
come from a sieve of segments of odd numbers, and the cap is 65536 primes.

The exponents of the primes of the base in each relation were a dense byte
matrix, relations by primes, which grows as the square of the base: 1.5 GB
at 38280 primes, 4.3 GB at 65250. Beyond 16384 primes, they are now kept
sparse: the exponents that are not 0, grouped by relation before the square
roots. Up to 16384 primes the dense matrix stays, faster there: the sparse
one took 1.06 of its time at 80 bits, 1.01 at 120. On an Apple M5 Pro,
`siqs-bench` on 3 balanced semiprimes per size, against the capped and
dense code:

| bits | 248 | 256 | 264 | 280 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| before | 17.6 s | 38.5 s | 69.2 s | 406 s |
| now | 14.9 s | 26.4 s | 40.0 s | 146 s |
| ratio | 0.85 | 0.69 | 0.58 | 0.36 |
| peak memory | 340 &rarr; 150 MB | 342 &rarr; 239 MB | 344 &rarr; 375 MB | 350 &rarr; 983 MB |

At 280 bits, most of the memory is the binary matrix of the elimination,
still dense (about 530 MB for 65250 primes); the sieve takes 90% to 96% of
a run from 248 to 280 bits, the linear algebra 3% to 9%. Below 240 bits,
where the cap did not apply, SIQS takes 0.996 to 1.008 of its former time
from 100 to 232 bits, and `tifa_factor()` 1.008 to 1.012 at 80 bits, where
the code moved. `test_siqs` factors with 34000 primes in the base, past the
table and the dense exponents; `test_matrix` checks the sparse exponents
against dense ones.

## 4 November 2026 &mdash; Security fixes

A review of the library for untrusted input (certificates, tuning files,
the tools' arguments, the Python package and its release) found these,
now fixed:

- `tifa_check_prime_cert()` divided by zero, killing the process, on a
  certificate with 0 among the primes of p - 1 or p + 1. It also checked
  every step before refusing those for primes above n, so that a certificate
  of 30 KB took 29 s, and searched the steps in time quadratic in their
  number. Each prime now has at most one step, the last for n and the
  others for primes below n, found in a sorted index. `tifa.check_certificate`
  follows the same rules, and refuses, as the C parser does, witnesses of
  2^32 or more and a D of 0 or of 2^31 or more in absolute value.
- `tifa_prime_cert_from_str()` read numbers in time quadratic in their
  length (7 s for a million digits), and `tifa_prime_cert_to_str()` read past
  the witnesses of a step that has fewer than primes.
- The Python wheels had the temporary directory of their build compiled in
  as the last place to look for tuning files: on Linux, a directory in /tmp
  that any user could create, to choose the parameters of everyone's
  `tifa.factor()`. They now have none (only `TIFA_TUNING_DIR`), so a new
  release is the fix for those installed.
- The tuning files were read whatever they were: a FIFO blocked the program,
  and /dev/zero made it spin, both with the lock of the tuning tables held.
  Only regular files are read now, of at most 4096 lines, with no null
  character; a directory too long for the path buffer no longer names
  another file; and setuid and setgid programs ignore `TIFA_TUNING_DIR`.
- The coprime base of the factors found recursed once per power of a
  prime, a stack overflow in a thread for 1009^10001 * 1013, now a loop;
  `process_found_factors()` divided by a factor 0 and looped forever on -3
  (it now drops 0, 1 and n, takes absolute values and refuses
  non-divisors); the binary searches of sorted arrays read past their end
  when the range given did; `split_u64()` took 32 s on a prime, `fermat()`
  45 s on 3, `rho()` with more accumulated than allowed iterations 258 s, and
  SIQS with the smallest factor base, above 140 bits, or a pool of more than
  255 primes for its coefficients, never ended. All return at once now.
- `tifa.factor()` raised `RuntimeError` when `tifa_factor()` gave up, rather
  than `FactorizationError`.
- `tifa-tune` truncated paths too long for its buffers silently.
- The workflows' token can no longer write to the repository, the actions
  are pinned to commits, and the checkouts keep no credentials; publishing
  to PyPI also waits for a maintainer's approval now, from a release tag
  only.

## 4 November 2026 &mdash; Primality certificates

`tifa_factor()` tells primes from composites by a Baillie-PSW test, which no
composite is known to pass, but which proves nothing above 2^64. The new
`tifa_prove_prime()` (`prime_cert.h`) proves that an integer is prime and
returns a certificate that `tifa_check_prime_cert()` checks, as anyone can
with modular exponentiations, Lucas sequences and GCDs. Each step proves a
prime p from primes of p - 1 and of p + 1, each below 2^64 or proven by an
earlier step:

- primes q of p - 1 with witnesses a make every prime factor of p 1 mod F1,
  the part of p - 1 they make (Pocklington);
- primes r of p + 1 with Lucas sequences of discriminant D, (D / p) = -1,
  make every prime factor s of p (D / s) mod F2, that is +-1 (Morrison);
- then p is prime if (F1 + 1)^2 > p, or F1^3 > p and a number is not a
  square (Brillhart, Lehmer and Selfridge), or lcm(F1, F2)^2 > p and the
  one residue left, p mod lcm(F1, F2), does not divide p.

Primes below 2^64 need no step: they are strong probable primes to the first
12 primes, which no composite below 2^64 is.

p - 1 is searched for factors by trial division and ECM (a twentieth of the
time SIQS would take), then p + 1 if they are not enough, and only then is
the smallest composite left factored by SIQS; the smallest primes found are
kept until p is proven, from p - 1 alone if it can. On an Apple M5 Pro,
proofs took, on average over random primes (100 per size, 60 at 224 bits,
40 at 256):

| bits | 128 | 160 | 192 | 224 | 256 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| p - 1 and p + 1 | 0.5 ms | 1.4 ms | 5.5 ms | 53 ms | 66 ms |
| p - 1 alone, by `tifa_factor()` | 0.4 ms | 3.0 ms | 15 ms | 212 ms | about 2 s |

The slowest at 224 bits took 1.1 s, rather than 2.4 s with p - 1 alone:
both p - 1 and p + 1 needed SIQS. A check takes less than 0.5 ms.

In Python, `tifa.factor(n, proof=True)` also proves the prime factors above
2^64 (2% to 25% more time on random numbers of 100 to 200 bits),
`tifa.prime_certificate(p)` returns the certificate as plain data,
`(p, [(q, a), ...])` or `(p, [(q, a), ...], D, [(r, P), ...])` steps, and
`tifa.check_certificate(p, cert)` checks it in plain Python, without TIFA's
C code. In C, `tifa_prime_cert_to_str()` writes a certificate in that
format, in JSON, a step per line, as `snprintf` writes a string, and
`tifa_prime_cert_from_str()` reads it back, or the same JSON otherwise
spaced (that of Python's `json.dumps`, for example), for
`tifa_check_prime_cert()` to check.
`tifa-factor --prove` proves the factors it finds, or the number if it is
prime, and prints their certificates so; a factor not proven prime makes
the factorization partial (exit status 1).

Nothing else changes: `tifa_factor()` does not use the new code, the
benchmark programs have the same machine code, and the code of the shared
library before it keeps its addresses. The ABI only grows.
