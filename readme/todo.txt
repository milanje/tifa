Here are some short term objectives:

  - Check tifa_cofactor() on real cofactors from a number field sieve (dumps
    from CADO-NFS, for example). So far it has only been measured on
    synthetic cofactors: integers without small factors, of the sizes a
    sieve leaves.

  - Machine-specific parameters. The defaults of SIQS from 160 to 220 bits
    were retuned on an Apple M5 Pro and on GitHub's x86-64 runners (0.4.3);
    sweeping the factor base, the sieve width and the threshold on both, in
    November 2026, found them within 2% of the best everywhere, the machines
    disagreeing on the rest (a larger factor base at 190 bits on x86-64, a
    smaller one at 210). Tuning files (tifa-tune) can take that last 2% to
    3% on each machine. From 232 bits the defaults come from a fit on rows
    tuned on the M5 at 272 and 288 bits, their sieve half widths halved from
    248 to 280 bits for the M5 and the newer x86-64 cores; an EPYC 7763
    (Zen 3) was up to 9% faster with twice as wide ones from 264 bits, for a
    reason not known (its 512 KB L2 cache?). tifa-tune --search descent
    --fit tunes these sizes on a machine.

  - SIQS above 280 bits: the binary matrix of the elimination is dense,
    about 530 MB at 65536 primes, the cap of the factor base, reached at
    288 bits; a sparse method (block Lanczos or Wiedemann) would be needed
    beyond. Above 512 bits, tifa_factor() searches with ecm() in GMP
    arithmetic up to factors of 85 bits (about 12 minutes at 520 bits, 30 at
    1024, when it finds nothing), then runs SIQS, which takes forever there:
    ECM with ever larger bounds would at least find larger factors.

  - ECM on one to four limbs (mont_ecm.c), measured on 30 October 2026:
      - The setup of a curve takes 0.3 us (Z/2 x Z/8) to 0.6 us (Suyama),
        3% to 6% of a curve with B1 = 50 on two limbs, 1% with B1 = 800;
        its inversion is half of that. ecm() keeps the plans of its levels
        from one call to the next (0.4.2), where building them took 1 to 2
        us each for B1 up to 200 (about 3% at 100 bits).
      - The baby and giant steps of stage 2 are chains of dependent xadd,
        13% of ecm() at 100 bits. As two chains each (steps of 4.P and of
        2.d.P), their xadd in interleaved pairs, ecm() took 0.955 to 0.98
        of its time from 70 to 128 bits on the M5, but 1.04 to 1.23 on
        x86-64 with GCC (Zen 3 and Zen 5): the pairs spill there. Not kept.
        GCC's inlining of add and sub in these kernels also shifts with the
        size of mont_ecm.c: forcing it slowed x86 by 13% to 15% on three
        and four limbs.
      - Not faster: a dedicated Montgomery squaring in the ladder step
        (0.97 to 1.00 of stage 1); two curves interleaved in one step (0.68
        on one limb, whose numbers go to factor_u64 instead, 0.87 to 0.92
        on two, 0.95 to 1.00 on three and four).
      - The levels of ecm_schedule.c (tifa_cofactor, tifa_factor's ECM
        search) were tuned for Suyama's curves; they run the Z/2 x Z/8 ones
        first since November 2026 (1% to 2.4% less work on sets of
        cofactors). Retuned for them (November 2026: mean curves to find a
        factor of exactly fbits bits, starting from curve 0, times the time
        of a curve on two limbs), every level from 25 to 60 bits is
        fastest at half to a third of its B1, 10% to 33% less expected time
        per level. Neither that table nor one keeping each level's time
        with the lower B1 (more curves) helped: on 20000 and 12000
        cofactors, 0.997 to 1.017 of the instructions exactly, and the
        first 1.08 to 1.26 when giving up (it spends more of the budget and
        gives up less); tifa_factor 0.86 to 1.03 over three seeds, within
        the spread of the curves' luck. Failures, bounded by the budget,
        and SIQS dominate these runs, not the levels' expected times.

  - SIQS, in shares of a run (November 2026, after 0.4.4): the sieve's fill
    55% to 75% from 160 to 220 bits, at about one cycle per hit on the M5;
    the bucket fill 15% at 220 bits on the M5, 19% to 20% on x86-64; at 180
    bits, the candidate pre-check and the candidates' values 6%, the batch
    smoothness test 7% to 9% and the trial division of the relations 3% to
    4%; the linear algebra 2% at 220 bits (FILTERED_GAUSS_ELIM).
      - The bucket fill with AVX-512 (0.4.6) compresses each vector of
        hits once per bin: from 225 bits, with 14 chunks per side or more,
        looping over the bins its lanes hit might keep more of its gain.
        Without AVX-512 the fill stays scalar. Bins of their own for the
        second roots, faster in a microbenchmark whose bins stayed in
        cache, were 1% to 3% slower in SIQS: measure such kernels with the
        caches flushed between runs.
      - The candidates the pre-check drops have their values u = a.x + b
        computed for nothing, and the trial division of the relations could
        start from the small primes the pre-check found: 1% to 2% at 180
        bits, perhaps.
      - The code of fill_sieve_chunk is sensitive to its layout on the M5:
        three instructions before its loops moved them and cost 3% at 150
        bits. Time 150 to 160 bits on the M5 after changing it.
      - Not faster: groups of medium primes with AVX2 (no better than SSE2)
        or NEON (slower on the M5); 128 KiB sieve chunks on the M5; testing
        128 bytes at a time in the scan; three-limb lanes in the batch test;
        trial division of every candidate instead of the batch test (1.06 of
        the time at 110 bits, 1.28 at 200), or testing them against the
        roots of their polynomial, four primes per NEON vector (not enough
        to pay for an x86 path); a retune of the parameters (see above);
        with the AVX-512 fill, buckets from 8192 to 32768 rather than 1.5
        chunks (49152), whose profile made them look cheaper per hit than
        the SSE2 groups: 1.00 to 1.03 of the time from 180 to 220 bits on
        two EPYC 9V74, 0.97 to 0.99 at 200 to 220 on an EPYC 9V45 (within
        2% of the noise there).
      - Double large primes (November 2026, a prototype counting the
        relations with two large primes and the cycles they would close, by
        union-find, on single numbers): none come through as SIQS stands,
        since the pre-check drops cofactors above log2(pmax) + 8 bits, and
        two primes above pmax need about twice that. Letting cofactors of up
        to 40 to 50 bits through, with large primes up to 15e6, 2^26 or 2^28,
        the cycles would have ended the run at 0.68 to 0.89 of the time of
        that run, but those runs took 1.5 to 2 times as long (the batch test
        on many more candidates, the splitting), so that the best of them
        would have taken 1.46 times the time of SIQS as it is at 200 bits,
        1.25 at 240, 1.18 at 264. Free candidates, the cycles would cut 25%
        to 32% of the sieving at 240 and 264 bits: double large primes need
        a much cheaper way to the cofactor of a candidate than the batch
        test (resieving, as msieve and yafu do), or larger numbers than
        SIQS takes here. A large prime bound of 2^26 rather than 15e6 alone
        gains 3% at 240 bits, within the noise.
      - Threads: the collection of relations would split over several cores
        (the library is thread-safe), for its wall-clock time.
      - From seven primes in 'a', the approximer sorts up to 18424 triples
        of primes per run (0.5 ms, 0.5% at 170 bits): pairs, which fixed six
        primes, gave slower 'a' there, but finding the best triple without
        the table would keep the same 'a'.
